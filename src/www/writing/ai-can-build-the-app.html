AI Can Build the App. The Business Still Has to Work.

Robert Guida, Fractional CTO & Solutions Architect

AI has given entrepreneurs a remarkable opportunity. Someone with a useful idea can now build a prototype, test a concept, and sometimes create an entire application without first financing a development team or learning to write every line themselves.

That is something to celebrate. More people get to try. More ideas get a chance.

But the cost of producing software is only one part of the cost of running a software business. That distinction deserves more attention in the conversation about AI, entrepreneurship, and the future of software developers.

It is easy to watch someone produce an application in an afternoon and jump to a much larger conclusion: everyone can now build their own software, entrepreneurs no longer need developers, and established software businesses are about to become unnecessary.

A working demonstration supports a much narrower conclusion. Something that once required considerable effort has become easier to produce.

What happens afterward still matters.

Someone still has to protect customer information, control access, maintain the software, monitor suspicious activity, recover from failures, and support the people relying on the service. The FTC’s guidance describes these responsibilities across development and ongoing operations. They extend well beyond getting the application to run. FTC: Start with Security

This is where a founder can underestimate the business they have entered. The app works. Hosting seems affordable. Customers are signing up. The visible evidence suggests success, while obligations that have never been tested remain out of sight.

Growth can make that gap larger. Every new account may represent another person whose information must be protected, another customer who expects support, and another obligation when something goes wrong.

The characters and amounts are fictional. The responsibilities are real, and the financial consequences are not theoretical.

After a breach exposed information about approximately 2.5 million consumers, the FTC took action against alcohol-delivery platform Drizly and its CEO personally. The FTC alleged that they had been alerted to security problems years earlier without adequately addressing them. Its final 2023 order imposed security obligations on the CEO for ten years, including at certain future businesses he owns or leads. This was personal regulatory accountability, rather than a personal damages award, but the obligations attached to the individual executive. FTC case page, final order

Following its 2020 breach, EyeMed agreed to $600,000 in New York penalties and a separate $5 million consumer class-action settlement. The same incident can bring both government enforcement and customer litigation. New York attorney general, court-authorized settlement website

Software provider Blackbaud agreed to a $49.5 million multistate settlement following its 2020 breach. Its annual report also disclosed $22.4 million in incident-related legal expenses during 2023 alone. The costs continued years after the original event. New York attorney general, Blackbaud’s annual report

These examples do not mean every breach produces a penalty, every lawsuit succeeds, or every founder becomes personally liable. They show the financial and legal responsibilities a software business can carry. Those responsibilities remain relevant however the code was written.

Insurance makes the economics more concrete. Among its U.S. technology customers, broker Insureon reports median premiums of approximately $110 a month for technology errors-and-omissions coverage and $179 a month for cyber insurance—roughly $1,320 and $2,148 annually. These are separate benchmarks, with possible coverage overlap, rather than a combined quote. Limits, deductibles, and exclusions still apply, and company coverage does not automatically protect a founder against every personal claim. Technology insurance benchmarks

Obtaining suitable coverage also involves scrutiny. Insurers examine the business and its security practices, including controls such as multifactor authentication and incident-response planning. The amount and sensitivity of personally identifiable information matter: the number of user records and whether they contain email addresses, financial details, or health information change the exposure. A rapidly growing app cannot assume that a premium quoted for a small, low-risk business applies to it. Eligibility, pricing, and coverage terms depend on the risk the insurer is being asked to accept. Underwriting requirements, data and pricing factors

This helps explain why “it takes money to make money” still has relevance. AI may reduce the initial development bill. The business still needs resources for reliable operation, customer acquisition, support, and setbacks. A founder can contribute unpaid time, but that time is an investment too.

And the investment can fail even if the application is secure and well run.

An entrepreneur may spend months building something, only to discover that customers prefer an existing product or that several competitors have arrived with similar ideas. Easier development can increase the opportunities to enter a market while also making entry easier for competitors.

Yet the ability to build does not automatically create the desire to own and operate software.

A business owner who could create a scheduling application might still prefer to pay for one. That purchase can include maintenance, support, reliability, and someone else taking responsibility for keeping the service available. Building it personally would add another commitment to an already busy working life.

The same distinction applies to entrepreneurship. Someone may enjoy turning an idea into an application and have little interest in marketing it, selling it, answering support requests, funding security work, or maintaining it for years.

Access to the tool does not supply that willingness.

This is why I think some of the excitement—and some of the fear—may be running ahead of the economics. There may be a bubble in expectations about how easily an application becomes a profitable business. More prototypes and more launches do not, by themselves, establish how many durable businesses will follow.

That leaves plenty of uncertainty for software developers. Some work may become cheaper, some purchasing decisions may change, and familiar roles may evolve. Experience is not a guarantee that any particular job will remain unchanged.

But a flood of demonstrations is incomplete evidence for the claim that experience has lost its value. Judgment about security, reliability, maintenance, and customer obligations remains part of what a software business needs.

AI has lowered an important barrier to entry. The barriers involving customers, capital, trust, and sustained responsibility still shape what happens next.

That is a reason for a calmer view of the situation. More people can attempt to build software. How many will want to run the resulting businesses—and how many of those businesses will succeed—is a much larger question.

See this moment differently. People who are building apps with AI, but have never created code one keystroke at a time, probably have no clue what lies ahead. But you do! Be the evangelist and try to save them from themselves.
